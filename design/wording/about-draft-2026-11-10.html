About these figures

  1. Roadteller counts collisions on public roads in which someone was injured and which were reported to the police, from the police's records published by the Department for Transport. Collisions in which nobody was hurt aren't included, and some injuries are never reported to the police, so the true numbers are higher.

  2. The main figures cover the latest five complete years, 2021 to 2025, and compare them with the five years before, 2016 to 2020. The earlier years include 2020, when there was much less traffic during the pandemic.

  3. A circle counts every collision the police recorded within half a mile, a mile or two miles of the middle of the postcode or place. A council area counts every collision within its present boundaries, whichever council it was recorded under at the time.

  4. "One injury collision for every 14.0 million miles" compares collisions on a council area's A-roads and motorways with the miles driven on them, from the Department for Transport's traffic figures. Minor roads aren't included, because their traffic is only estimated for whole areas.

  5. Traffic is measured stretch by stretch, between junctions. Only about a quarter of the yearly figures for A-roads and motorways come from a count on that road in that year; the rest are the Department for Transport's estimates, and about a third of stretches weren't counted at all from 2021 to 2025. The Department says figures for a single road or a small area are less reliable than those for a region or the whole country, so a road's figure is a guide rather than an exact measure.

    DfT asks anyone publishing its road-by-road traffic figures to note their limits (roadtraffic.dft.gov.uk/about, "Data disclaimer"). Figures from DfT's 2021 to 2025 file, A-roads and motorways: 24% of yearly figures counted; 6,999 of 19,073 count points not counted in any of the five years.

  6. Each collision on an A-road or motorway is matched to the nearest stretch of the same road, drawn along the real road from Ordnance Survey's map. One that happened within 60 metres of a junction between two stretches counts half to each. About one in seven collisions on these roads falls at a junction like this.

    The build's figures for 2021 to 2025: 33,746 of 242,499 collisions on A-roads and motorways at a junction between stretches (14%); 1.3% couldn't be matched.

  7. A rate is only given where at least 10 collisions lie behind it, as fewer are too few to compare fairly.

  8. "Allowing for the mix of roads" takes the Great Britain rate for motorways and for A-roads, applies each to the miles driven on that kind of road in the area, and compares the result with the collisions there. Motorways have far fewer collisions for their traffic than other roads, so an area with a lot of motorway would otherwise look better than it is.

  9. "Better or worse than before?" goes back to 2000 for rates. For a circle, counts go back to 1989. Before then, the locations of too many collisions can't be placed on a map. Older locations are also rougher, often to the nearest 100 metres, so a collision near the edge of a circle may fall just inside or just outside it.

  10. "Serious or fatal" is as the police recorded it. Since some police forces changed how they record injuries, more injuries are recorded as serious than before, so changes over time, or differences between areas, can partly reflect how they were recorded.

    gov.uk "Road safety open data": "The introduction of injury-based reporting of casualty severity for some police forces appears to have led to a change in the reported severity of road casualties."

  11. The map's squares count collisions, they don't allow for traffic. Busy roads have more collisions because more people use them, so a busy junction shows darker even where it is no more dangerous than a quiet lane.

    Your note from the map options, with a sentence added; "dangerous" is used here only to say the map doesn't measure it.

  12. Roadteller shows totals only. It never lists individual collisions or the people involved.
